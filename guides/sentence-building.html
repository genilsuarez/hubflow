How an English sentence is assembled, how to put scrambled words back in order, and how to fuse two short sentences into one.

🧩 The Core Order

English word order is rigid. The subject stays, the verb follows, and the extras have fixed slots.
SubjectVerbObjectMannerPlaceTime
Shereadthe letterquietlyin the kitchenlast night
Weplayfootball—at the parkon Sundays
I speak English well.
I speak well English.

Nothing separates a verb from its direct object. Adverbs of manner go after the object, never between verb and object.

Time can move to the front for emphasis — Last night she read the letter — but place and manner cannot: In the kitchen she read the letter is possible but marked, and Quietly she read the letter is literary.

🔄 Putting Scrambled Words Back

A repeatable procedure instead of guessing.
  1. Find the verb. It anchors everything else.
  2. Find who does it — that is the subject, and it goes first.
  3. Attach the object immediately after the verb.
  4. Slot the extras in manner → place → time order.
  5. Check the capital letter and the full stop — they tell you which word starts and ends.
Scrambled
yesterday / the / to / went / park / we
Rebuilt
We went to the park yesterday.
Question word order is different: the auxiliary jumps before the subject — Where did you go? If the scrambled set contains did, do, is or a wh-word, build a question, not a statement.

🔗 Combining Two Sentences Into One

Four moves cover almost every combining task.
Coordination
Equal weight: and, but, so, or.
It rained. We stayed in.
→ It rained, so we stayed in.
Subordination
One idea becomes background: because, although, when, if.
→ Because it rained, we stayed in.
Relative clause
Merge a repeated noun with who / which / that.
The man called. He lives next door.
→ The man who lives next door called.
Participle
Same subject, shorter result.
She opened the door. She saw him.
→ Opening the door, she saw him.
Although he was tired, he kept working.
Although he was tired, but he kept working.

One connector per link. Spanish allows aunque… pero…; English treats the second connector as a repetition and marks it wrong.
