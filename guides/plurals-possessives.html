Plurals & Possessives

Two endings that look identical and mean opposite things. A plural counts more than one; an apostrophe shows who owns what. Get the difference and the rest is spelling.

📚 Regular plurals — five spellings

The noun ends in…AddExamples
anything else+ sbook → books • student → students
-s, -x, -ch, -sh+ esbox → boxes • dish → dishes • watch → watches
consonant + yy → iesbaby → babies • city → cities
vowel + y+ sday → days • boy → boys • key → keys
-f / -fef → vesknife → knives • leaf → leaves
💡 The y rule depends on the letter before the y
Look at the letter before the y, not the y itself. A consonant before it (baby, city) means y → ies. A vowel before it (day, boy) means the y stays and you just add -s.
city → cities ✓ day → daies ✗ day → days ✓

🌀 Irregular plurals — memorise these

TypeSingular → Plural
Different wordperson → people • child → children
Vowel change a → eman → men • woman → women
Vowel change oo → eefoot → feet • tooth → teeth • goose → geese
Vowel change ou → imouse → mice • louse → lice
No change at allsheep → sheep • fish → fish • deer → deer
👀 Written vs spoken
Women changes its first vowel sound, not the one you changed in writing: /ˈwʊmən/ → /ˈwɪmɪn/. And people is already plural — never say "peoples" for a group of persons.
three people ✓ three peoples ✗ three persons ✗

✍️ Possessive 's — who owns it

The owner is…AddExamples
singular'sTom's car • the girl's bag
plural ending in -s' onlymy parents' house • the students' books
plural NOT ending in -s'schildren's toys • men's team • people's opinions
a name ending in -s's or 'James's car = James' car
✅ One question decides it
Ask: does the owner already end in -s? If yes and it's a plural, put the apostrophe after that s and stop. Otherwise add 's. The apostrophe always goes immediately after the owner — that's how a reader knows how many owners there are.
my sister's room = one sister my sisters' room = two or more
❌ The mistake almost everyone makes
A plural on its own never takes an apostrophe. If nothing is being owned, there is nothing to mark. The -s alone is enough.
I have two dogs. ✓ I have two dog's. ✗ The dog's tail. ✓ (the tail of the dog)

🤔 The same 's, three meanings

Written English uses one ending for three different things. Expanding it out loud tells you which one you have.

followed by adjective / noun / -ing
's = is
The short form of is.
Sara's happy. → Sara is happy.
It's raining. → It is raining.
followed by a past participle or "got"
's = has
The short form of has.
She's finished. → She has finished.
He's got a car. → He has got a car.
followed by a noun that belongs to it
's = possession
Not short for anything — it marks the owner.
Sara's book. → the book of Sara. ("Sara is book" and "Sara has book" are both impossible.)
✅ The substitution test
Say the sentence with is. If it doesn't work, say it with has. If neither makes sense, the 's is a possessive.
John's tired → John is tired (is) John's left → John has left (has) John's car → neither (possession)
💡 One sentence can hold two of them
Test each 's separately — they don't have to match.
That's my brother's jacket. = That is my brother's jacket.
