🌟 Subjunctive & Unreal Forms

A past form that does not mean past time — it marks distance from reality.
TriggerFormExample
wish / if onlypast for now, past perfect for regretI wish I knew. / I wish I had known.
as if / as thoughpast for unreal comparisonHe talks as if he owned the place.
it's timepastIt's time we left.
would rather (someone)pastI'd rather you didn't smoke.
suppose / imaginepastSuppose you won — what then?
demand / insist / suggestbare infinitive (mandative)They insisted he be present.
were for all persons in the unreal: If I were you… / He behaves as if he were in charge. Was is heard in speech but were is the form expected in writing and in the fixed phrase if I were you.
wish + would is for other people's annoying habits, not your own states: I wish he would stop interrupting, but I wish I had more time — never I wish I would have.

↩️ Future in the Past

Talking about what was still ahead at a past moment.
Present formPast-viewpoint formExample
willwouldHe said he would come.
be going towas going toShe was going to resign, but she stayed.
present continuouspast continuousWe were leaving the next morning.
be about towas about toI was about to call when he arrived.
be towas toHe was to become prime minister.

was going to and was to have often carry the implication that it never happened: We were going to meet (but we didn't); The flight was to have left at six (it didn't).

⌛ Aspect & Time Nuance

Same time, different viewpoint. Aspect is a choice, not a rule.
PairDifference
I've lived here 5 years / I've been living here 5 yearsfact vs the ongoing, possibly temporary experience
I've read the report / I've been reading the reportfinished vs unfinished, effort visible
She was rude / She was being rudecharacter vs behaviour on that occasion
He had left / He had been leavingcompleted before vs repeated attempts
I forgot to lock it / I had forgotten to lock itevent vs state discovered later
Continuous suggests limits. It implies temporariness, incompleteness or repetition — which is why it clashes with state verbs and with permanent facts.

✂ Ellipsis & Substitution

Fluent English omits what is recoverable, and replaces the rest with a pro-form.
Verb-phrase ellipsis
She can swim and he can too.
(not he can swim too)
do so / do it
He was asked to resign, and he did so.
one / ones
I'll take the red one.
so / not
— Is he coming? — I think so. / I'm afraid not.
to (bare)
I didn't want to go, but I had to.
Gapping
Ana ordered fish; Luis, steak.
Only omit what the reader can restore exactly. If two candidates could fill the gap, the sentence is ambiguous, not economical.
